## 1. Accessories earn the least revenue but have by far the best margin

**Finding:** Accessories bring in only 4.46% of completed revenue (KSh 1,077,496.10), the smallest share of the five categories (Q16, Q42). But their profit margin is 42.39%, about four times the other categories, which sit between 9.92% (Electronics) and 11.38% (Home Appliances) (Q33). On roughly KSh 1.08M of sales, accessories earn about KSh 457,000 in profit, as much as Home Appliances, which sells almost four times more.

**Why it matters:** Revenue alone ranks accessories last, so they are easy to ignore. In profit terms, each shilling of accessory sales is worth about four shillings of phone or laptop sales. The category the business pays least attention to is its most efficient one.

**Recommendation:** Sell accessories alongside phones and laptops: bundle a case, charger or earphones with every handset and laptop, and display them at the checkout. Don't discount accessories to win a sale, because a 42% margin can absorb a small price cut on the main item instead. Track accessories attached per phone sold as a monthly measure.

## 2. Over half of revenue comes from two categories that earn only about 10% margin

**Finding:** Mobile Phones (28.74%) and Computing (27.31%) together bring in 56.05% of completed revenue (Q42), but their margins are only 10.5% and 10.61% (Q33). Electronics, the third-largest category at 22.83% of revenue, has the thinnest margin of all at 9.92%.

**Why it matters:** The business's sales volume depends on categories with thin margins. A small rise in supplier cost, or a few extra points of discount, takes a large share of profit. In the three biggest categories, a 2-point discount increase would cut the margin by about a fifth.

**Recommendation:** Review discounting on Mobile Phones, Computing and Electronics first, and set a minimum margin on discounted orders (for example, no discount that takes a product below about 8%). Negotiate supplier prices on the highest-volume products, since a small cost saving there adds more profit than a large gain in a small category.

## 3. Revenue is growing fast: Jan to Sep 2026 is up 86% on the same months of 2025

**Finding:** Completed revenue for January to September 2026 is KSh 12.83M, against KSh 6.89M for the same nine months of 2025, a rise of about 86% (Q40, Q43). August 2026 was the best month in the data at KSh 2,242,033.90, more than double August 2025 (KSh 1,056,881.40). Every month from February 2026 onward is well above its 2025 equivalent.

**Why it matters:** The growth is steady across most months, so it doesn't come from one lucky month. Stock, staff and cash flow planned for 2025's volume will be too small for 2026's.

**Recommendation:** Plan 2027 stock and staffing against the 2026 run rate (about KSh 1.4M a month), not 2025. Review supplier terms and delivery capacity in the busiest towns before the next peak.

## 4. Sales swing sharply with the calendar: December peaks, then January collapses

**Finding:** December 2025 earned KSh 2,027,120.60, about 18% of the whole year's revenue, and January 2026 fell 84.09% to KSh 322,432.95, the lowest month in the data (Q40). The first two months of 2025 were also the weakest of that year (KSh 434,902.70 and KSh 369,565.60). Revenue also jumped in July 2025 (+59.91%) and June 2026 (+72.97%).

**Why it matters:** A business that earns a large share of its year in one month and very little right after faces a cash squeeze in January, when rent, salaries and supplier bills are still due. With only one December in the data, treat this as a pattern to watch, not a proven rule.

**Recommendation:** Build a cash reserve from December sales to cover January and February. Run a planned January offer (for example, a trade-in or a bundle promotion) so the slump isn't left to chance. Re-check the pattern after December 2026.

## 5. A third of customers bring in 78% of revenue

**Finding:** The 43 High Value customers (spending KSh 180,000 or more) generate KSh 18.84M, about 78% of completed revenue. The 49 Medium customers bring in KSh 4.57M (about 19%), and the 40 Low Value customers bring in only KSh 0.75M (about 3%) (Q45). An average High customer spends about KSh 438,000, against about KSh 93,000 for Medium and KSh 19,000 for Low.

**Why it matters:** The business depends on a small group. Losing a handful of High customers would cost more than losing the whole Low band several times over, while the Low band takes the same effort to serve.

**Recommendation:** Give High customers a named contact, early access to stock and loyalty pricing, and check weekly whether any of them has gone quiet (see Q34). Set a target to move Medium customers upward, since even a modest rise in their spending is worth far more than recruiting more Low-value customers.

## 6. The company keeps about 12 cents of every shilling, so discounts are expensive

**Finding:** Completed revenue is KSh 24,155,371.70 and profit is KSh 2,890,921.70, an overall margin of about 12% (Q13, Q33). One percent of revenue is about KSh 241,500, which equals roughly 8% of total profit.

**Why it matters:** With a margin this thin, small discounts or cost rises take a large share of profit. Strong revenue growth (insight 3) does not help if it is bought with price cuts.

**Recommendation:** Track margin by month alongside revenue, and set a minimum margin for discounted orders. Direct sales effort toward high-margin items such as accessories (insight 1) rather than adding volume in thin-margin categories.

## 7. Nairobi alone brings in about 40% of revenue, and two cities bring in more than half

**Finding:** Nairobi customers generated KSh 9,581,980.35, or 39.7% of completed revenue. Adding Mombasa (KSh 3,161,889.60, 13.1%) brings the two cities to 52.8%. At the other end, Machakos earned KSh 745,099.90 (3.1%), so Nairobi sells almost 13 times as much. Thika, Nyeri and Machakos together bring in 12.0% (Q25).

**Why it matters:** The business depends heavily on one market. A local problem in Nairobi, such as a competitor opening nearby or a supply delay, would hit overall revenue far harder than losing several smaller towns. The weakest towns are also the least developed markets, with room to grow.

**Recommendation:** Protect Nairobi first with stock availability and fast delivery. Then test a targeted campaign in one weak town, such as Machakos or Nyeri (for example a launch promotion for phones, the best-selling category), and compare its revenue after three months before spending in all three. Check customer counts per town (Q17) first, because a low-revenue town with few customers needs recruitment, while one with many customers needs better sales per customer.

## 8. VIP customers spend 55% more than Retail customers, but the gap is smaller than the segment names suggest

**Finding:** The average VIP customer has spent KSh 253,251.88, against KSh 197,958.71 for Corporate and KSh 163,575.21 for Retail, so a VIP customer is worth about 1.55 times a Retail customer (Q27). Compare this with the spending bands in Q45, where an average High customer spends about KSh 438,000, more than 20 times a Low customer.

**Why it matters:** The VIP label separates customers far less sharply than actual spending does. Some VIP customers will be low spenders, and some Retail customers will be big ones. If VIP perks are given by label, the business may be rewarding the wrong people and missing high-value Retail customers.

**Recommendation:** Review who is in the VIP segment against actual spending, and move customers into and out of it using the spending thresholds from Q45 (for example KSh 180,000 or more). Give loyalty benefits to anyone who reaches the High band, whatever their label.

## 9. Nearly a fifth of past revenue comes from customers who have stopped buying, including 8 High Value ones

**Finding:** 47 of the 132 purchasing customers (35.6%) have not completed an order in more than 90 days before the end of the data on 30 September 2026. Together they spent KSh 4,595,912.95, which is 19.0% of all completed revenue (Q34). Eight of them are High Value customers (KSh 180,000 or more), so about 1 in 5 of the 43 High customers has gone quiet (Q45).

**Why it matters:** This is revenue that may already be lost. The High Value customers matter most, because as insight 5 shows, that band brings in about 78% of revenue. Winning back one of them is worth far more than recruiting several Low Value customers. The silent group also averages about KSh 97,800 each, which is above the Medium band's average, so the loss is not limited to small buyers.

**Recommendation:** Contact the 8 High Value silent customers personally this month, with a call or a targeted offer, and ask why they stopped. Then send a win-back message to the remaining 39. Set up a standing monthly report using the Q34 query, so a customer is flagged when they pass 60 days without an order, before they reach 90.
